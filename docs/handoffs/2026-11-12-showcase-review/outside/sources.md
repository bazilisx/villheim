## Historical rooms

- **Viking age longhouse** (Hurstwic, "The Viking Longhouse",
  https://hurstwic.org/history/articles/daily_living/text/longhouse.htm): 5 to 7 m wide, 15 to 75 m long; two rows of
  posts make three aisles; a central corridor of packed earth; a long fire pit or fire circles per room in the middle;
  raised plank benches along both sides between the posts and the walls, for sitting, eating, working and sleeping,
  covered with sheepskins, with storage under them; the master and mistress in a closed bed closet, others on the
  benches; a pantry for food brought to the fire to cook; chests for goods; trestle tables put up for meals and taken
  down; boards and trestles stored on the cross beams; stools and a vertical loom; no windows, light from smoke holes,
  open doors and oil lamps; an entry room or double doors against drafts.
- **Seating in the hall** (Hurstwic, "Seating Arrangements",
  https://www.hurstwic.com/history/articles/society/text/seating.htm): two raised benches face each other across the
  long fire; the high seat is the middle of the upper bench, held by the head of the house; the honored guest sits in
  the middle of the lower bench, across the fire; rank falls with distance from the high seat and toward the door;
  corner seats and bench ends are lowest; extra guests on temporary benches on the floor; cushions, stools and a
  footstool by the high seat.
- **Medieval great hall** (World History Encyclopedia, "Great Hall", https://www.worldhistory.org/Great_Hall/): a
  raised dais with the high table at one end, the least drafty spot; the entry at the other end behind a screen with
  an opening at each side; passages from the screens end to the kitchen, pantry and private rooms; a hearth in the
  middle on a ground floor, against a wall with a hood on an upper floor; long trestle tables with benches round the
  sides; a permanent table a sign of standing; rushlights, oil lamps, candles on walls and iron candle stands; wall
  hangings and weapons on the walls.
- A talk on surviving medieval inns (Triskele Heritage,
  https://triskeleheritage.triskelepublishing.com/2024/02/20/exploring-the-mediaeval-inn/) was found, but the page
  only announces it; no layout facts.

## Real-world layout and decorating

- **Clearances** (Houzz, "Measurements to Know: A Guide for Furniture Layout and Ergonomics",
  https://www.houzz.com.sg/ideabooks/109239110): a seating group fits in an arc about 244 cm across; 61 cm between
  pieces of furniture to move between them; a walkway 77 to 91 cm; 91 cm behind a seated diner; 50 to 55 cm at the
  sides and foot of a double bed; a single bed may have one side to the wall; kitchen work points within 6.4 m in all.
- **Feng shui** (Cliff Tan, architect, in NPR Life Kit, "Feng shui inspired design tricks",
  https://www.npr.org/2024/07/19/g-s1-12312/feng-shui-inspired-design-tricks-transform-your-home): keep the entry
  clear ("the most important part of the home"); deal with the worst problem first; the command position: a solid
  wall behind you, the bed against a solid wall, feet not in line with an opening, head not beside a door; several
  low warm lights in place of one harsh light; flow through the room; furniture sized to the room (a round table lost
  in a long room; a coffee table too big to walk round).
- **Rule of odds** (Tidbits and Twine, "The Rule of Odds",
  https://www.tidbitsandtwine.com/rule-of-odds-interior-design/): odd groups have a middle for the eye; vary height,
  texture and shape inside a group (tall, medium, low); leave space inside a group; a tray or a stack holds small
  things as one unit; exceptions: formal symmetry, a single statement piece, functional pairs such as bedside tables
  and place settings.
- **Mixing woods** (Apartment Therapy, "This Is the Only Rule You Need to Know When Mixing Wood Tones", Emily
  Henderson, https://www.apartmenttherapy.com/mixing-woods-rule-according-to-a-designer-37263516; and "Rule of threes
  for wood tones", https://www.apartmenttherapy.com/rule-of-threes-for-wood-tones-37537091): match the undertone
  (warm, cool, neutral); let the largest wood (floor or the biggest piece) lead; no more than three distinct wood
  tones in a room, as base, contrast and accent; a fourth tone made a room "noisy" and swapping it for one that
  matched calmed it.
- **Mixing metals** (Homes and Gardens, Jordan Samson,
  https://www.homesandgardens.com/interior-design/jordan-samson-mixing-metals-tips): two to three metals per room, one
  or two in a small room; pick pairs with clear contrast (avoid near matches such as nickel with chrome); give all
  fittings of one function the same metal.
- **60-30-10** (Bob Vila, https://bobvila.com/articles/60-30-10-rule): about 60 % dominant color on the largest
  surfaces (walls, floor, big rugs and furniture), 30 % secondary (curtains, upholstery, art), 10 % accent (small
  items, metal finishes); a guide, not exact.
- **Layered light** (Redfin, "How to Use the Layered Lighting Technique",
  https://www.redfin.com/blog/how-to-use-layered-lighting/): general, task, accent and decorative light together;
  task light at desks, counters and bedsides; lights at varied heights, between the waist and just above eye level;
  lamps in corners to soften the edges; warm light.
- **What makes rooms less cozy** (Homes and Gardens, "6 things designers say are making your home less cozy",
  https://homesandgardens.com/interior-design/things-designers-say-make-homes-less-cozy): keep beams, stone and
  uneven floors on show (Lauren Gilberthorpe); layered materials and textures (Louise Copeland); layered warm light,
  never cold bright light (Gilberthorpe, Gaia Guidi Filippi); fabric at windows and a rug, or a room feels "like an
  icebox" (Tori Murphy); warm colors in dim rooms (Marianne Shillingford); seating sized to fill about a third of the
  room, leaving room for side tables, lamps and walking (Tracy Morris).

## Game environment art and storytelling

- **Don Carson, "Environmental Storytelling: Creating Immersive 3D Worlds Using Lessons Learned from the Theme Park
  Industry"** (Game Developer,
  https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/environmental-storytelling-creating-immersive-3d-worlds-using-lessons-learned-from-the-theme-park-industry):
  set the story's rules early and never break them; cause and effect vignettes that imply past events; show time
  passing; familiar reference points; contrast in light and order, and one unique pillar in a repeating row; save
  the best detail for the end of a space to pull people forward; leave areas in shadow and light what matters; less is
  more; one lit prop in a sparse room can carry more than a crowded room.
- **"3D Environment Artist on What Makes Environments Believable"** (80.lv,
  https://80.lv/articles/3d-environment-artist-on-what-makes-environments-believable/): wear comes from events, in the
  place the event would cause it; never add detail to fill space; every object has a reason (how it got there, how it
  is used); props share one story, or the scene is a museum of objects on display; a chair pulled back shows someone
  sat there; "Props are placed according to usage logic, not grid alignment."
- **"Cozy Clutter: Working on a Lived-In Hobbit Hole Interior"** (80.lv,
  https://80.lv/articles/cozy-clutter-working-on-a-lived-in-hobbit-hole-interior): prop stacks show someone lives
  there; small scenes such as a book on the armchair with a mug beside it, a candle melted onto a book; ask how the
  person used each object; wear where use happens (worn rug at the entry); a full room that still keeps the walk
  clear; build order structure, surfaces, furniture, small things; inks at the desk, papers stored in one corner;
  light first, warm.
- **"Creating Narrative in Interior Scenes"** (80.lv, https://80.lv/articles/creating-narrative-in-interior-scenes):
  decide the story early; one clear clue in a prominent place such as the fireplace; drop props that make a group
  busy; every light has a believable source; one accent color ties the room together.
- **"Breathing Life into Environments: Tips and Tricks"** (80.lv,
  https://80.lv/articles/breathing-life-into-environments-tips-tricks/): a hierarchy of large pieces, a few
  mid-sized pieces round them, then small detail; avoid many similar small items or many large ones alone; vary how
  crowded each area is; a strong first read on entry, then secondary and tertiary detail; calm areas where the eye
  rests; a theme set early keeps the props from looking assembled at random.
- **Dan Cox, GDC 2014, "What Modern Interior Design Teaches Us About Environment Art"** (Game Developer summary,
  https://gamedeveloper.com/design/video-how-environment-artists-can-learn-from-modern-interior-design): the summary
  names the subject only; the talk video was not watched.
- **Witcher 3** (80.lv, "The Witcher 3 tips for the environment design",
  https://80.lv/articles/the-witcher-3-tips-for-the-environment-design): story drives the layout; quest places keep
  their decoration on theme. Julius Peters' portfolio page (https://dvids.artstation.com/projects/2El8K) is a video
  only. The Witcher wiki page for Corvo Bianco has no interior pictures.
- **Kingdom Come: Deliverance** (Game Banshee interview with historian Joanna Nowak, https://gamebanshee.com/k3w4d):
  nothing on interior dressing; a historian supplied sources to artists, and some known errors (benches in churches)
  stayed for gameplay.
- **Enshrouded** (4Netplayers, https://www.4netplayers.com/en/blog/enshrouded/enshrouded-building-decoration-tips):
  columns to separate rooms; glowing blocks as small accents only; free rotation to break the grid; wood for warmth,
  stone for contrast.
- **ESO**: the UESP copy of "Learn the Basics of Housing and Update 43's Home Tours"
  (https://en.uesp.net/wiki/General:Learn_the_Basics_of_Housing_%26_Update_43%27s_Home_Tours, read in Tyler's Chrome)
  only links to three video guides (Kalam0n, CozyCaster, JakeClips); the videos were not watched. No ESO pictures
  were read this round; the earlier forum notes stand.
